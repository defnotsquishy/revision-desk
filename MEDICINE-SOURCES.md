# Medicine Through Time — notes and sources

Updated 10 October 2026. These 60 original revision cards replace the earlier
Medicine deck. They adapt the supplied notes into plain-language recall prompts,
with 12 cards each for medieval, Renaissance, industrial, modern medicine and
the British Western Front. Thirty cards also have independently written
multiple-choice questions. This is revision material, not a copied examination
paper, an official mark scheme or a guarantee of complete specification coverage.

## Course scope

[Pearson Edexcel GCSE History specification, Option 11](https://qualifications.pearson.com/content/dam/pdf/GCSE/History/2016/specification-and-sample-assessments/gcse-9-1-history-specification.pdf):
Medicine in Britain, c1250–present, with the British sector of the Western Front,
1914–18. The four chronological periods cover ideas, prevention, treatment,
care and factors for change; the historic environment considers conditions,
injuries and medical care on the Western Front. Henry VIII and Cold War cards
are not replaced by these notes.

## Corrections made while adapting the notes

- Medieval anatomy study was restricted and uncommon, but dissection was not
  universally illegal. Hospitals provided practical care as well as religious
  support, and some barber-surgeons learnt through apprenticeship.
  [Wellcome: anatomy](https://wellcomecollection.org/stories/getting-under-the-skin),
  [Wellcome: the medieval Church](https://wellcomecollection.org/stories/health-and-the-medieval-church).
- Jenner's experiment was in 1796; his findings appeared in 1798. Smallpox
  vaccination became compulsory in England and Wales in 1853.
  [Science Museum: Edward Jenner](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/people/cp37470/edward-jenner).
- The 1848 Public Health Act was mainly enabling; the 1875 Act imposed stronger
  sanitation duties. These were not identical interventions.
  [Parliament: 1848 Act](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/towncountry/towns/tyne-and-wear-case-study/about-the-group/public-administration/the-1848-public-health-act/),
  [Parliament: public-health dates](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/towncountry/towns/keydates/).
- Nightingale's work improved nursing and hospital care, but the Crimean
  mortality decline should not be attributed to her alone: sanitation and sewer
  repairs also mattered.
  [National Army Museum: Florence Nightingale](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/florence-nightingale-lady-lamp).
- Salvarsan was developed in 1909 and introduced in 1910. It was a synthetic
  treatment for syphilis, not penicillin.
  [Science Museum: Salvarsan](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co8102612/sealed-glass-tube-of-arsphenamine-salvarsan-606-europe-1910-1930).
- Modern routine vaccination should not be called compulsory. Diphtheria
  immunisation expanded in the 1940s; national polio and tetanus vaccination
  dates are 1956 and 1961. Published diphtheria timelines vary between the start
  of the campaign and programme dates, so no ambiguous exact-date quiz is used.
  [UKHSA: vaccination timeline](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/vaccination-timeline/vaccination-timeline-from-1796-to-present),
  [Hansard: the 1940 campaign](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/1953/jul/27/diphtheria-immunisation).
- England's smoke-free rules for enclosed workplaces/public places started on
  1 July 2007 under the Health Act 2006, not a blanket ban on smoking in 2006.
  [Original smoke-free regulations](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2006/3368/body/made).
- The Human Genome Project ran from 1990 to 2003. Germ theory explains
  infectious disease; not all illnesses are caused by microbes.
  [NHGRI: Human Genome Project timeline](https://www.genome.gov/human-genome-project/timeline).

## Western Front checks

- Second Ypres saw the first successful large-scale chlorine attack on
  22 April 1915; it was not the first use of any chemical weapon.
  [National Army Museum: wartime scientific advances](https://ww1.nam.ac.uk/1248/news/scientific-advances-first-world-war/).
- Thomas splints stabilised fractured femurs and greatly reduced mortality.
  A universal survival percentage for all wounded soldiers would be misleading.
  [National Army Museum: Thomas splint](https://collection.nam.ac.uk/detail.php?acc=2014-01-16--1).
- Oswald Hope Robertson's work with advance blood storage belongs to
  1917 and Cambrai. Citrate prevented clotting; preservation and cooling made
  stored blood available near the front. Transfusion counts are not unit counts.
  [Robertson's original 1918 paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2340627/),
  [US Army medical history](https://achh.army.mil/history/book-wwii-blood-chapter1/).
- Gillies and his team used reconstructive surgery, skin grafting and the tubed
  pedicle at Sidcup. The notes' claim of 12,000 operations per year is omitted.
  [Royal College of Surgeons: Gillies case files](https://www.rcseng.ac.uk/library-and-publications/library/blog/sir-harold-gillies-patient-case-files/).
- Shell shock was a varied contemporary diagnosis, not an exact synonym for
  every modern diagnosis of PTSD; responses to sufferers differed.
  [National Archives: original War Office report](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/education/resources/medicine-on-the-western-front-part-two/war-office-report-on-shell-shock/).

## Progress and printing

The new questions use new card IDs so old confidence is not assigned to different
questions. Existing rating records and dated graph points are not deleted.
Only current cards contribute to current deck coverage.

In Flashcards, expand **Print flashcards** and choose **Preview printable cards**.
The default is the whole deck, regardless of study mode. You can explicitly use
your current filters or leave answers out. Print/save-PDF uses the browser's
print window and an ink-efficient A4 handout, without private account details.
Printing never changes study progress. Source pages retain their own rights;
links are provided for checking facts, not as permission to republish them.
